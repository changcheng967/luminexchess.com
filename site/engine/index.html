Engine Inside

Two evaluations, one search

Luminex pairs a quantized neural network with a hand-crafted fallback, behind an alpha-beta search that orders, reduces and prunes its moves.

A real search, growing

1. Thinking

Watch it think

Real searches by v6.7.0's release build, on one thread of an Intel Xeon Platinum 8269CY, from positions of its wins in the gauntlet that rated it, replayed at their own pace. Each iteration searches one ply deeper; the line it expects lights the board, and the counters run as fast as the engine ran.

2. The parts

Six things it is made of

01Neural evaluation
A 768-wide HalfKAv2_hm network with 32 king buckets, its piece-square table held once for each of three phases of the game, 55,960 relation inputs beside it, and eight output buckets, chosen by the number of pieces. Its int16 accumulators follow every move incrementally, and the int8 layers behind them have kernels for AVX-512 VNNI, AVX-512, AVX-VNNI, AVX2, SSE and NEON, every one giving the same evaluation.
02Selective search
Principal variation search with aspiration windows, null-move pruning, singular extensions, ProbCut, razoring, futility and late-move pruning, and late-move reductions.
03Move ordering that learns
Killer and counter moves, continuation and capture history, static exchange evaluation, and correction history that tunes static scores from what the search finds.
04Many threads
Lazy SMP: each search thread keeps its own network accumulators and shares one transposition table with the others.
05Always a fallback
Every release since v6.5.1 has its network inside. If no network loads, a hand-crafted evaluation plays instead: material, piece-square tables, mobility, passed pawns and king safety, a feature set that follows Stash's, with values fitted for Luminex. The engine says so in an info line rather than switching quietly.
06Speaks UCI
Load it in Cute Chess, Arena, Banksia, En Croissant or any other UCI interface. MultiPV, pondering, win/draw/loss output and Polyglot opening books are built in.

Where the parts come from: some of the foundations derive from Stockfish, and the correction history, the history pruning rule, several search constants and the hand-crafted evaluation's feature set from Stash, by Morgan Houppin. Both are free under the GPL v3, as Luminex is, and the LICENSE credits them. Parts that derive from them are being replaced one at a time, each held to the same bench and perft or to an SPRT: so far the pins, the slider attack tables, the move list and the legal-move filter (#144), and the UCI layer (#145).

3. The search

How the search spends its time

An illustration of the ideas, not a real search tree

  1. 1

    Order the moves

    The move stored in the hash table goes first, then winning captures, killer moves, and quiet moves ranked by their history. A good first guess makes everything after it cheaper.

  2. 2

    Search the first move fully

    Principal variation search gives it the whole window of scores. Its result is the score every other move has to beat.

  3. 3

    Test the rest with a null window

    A later move only has to show it is no better, and a zero-width search answers that quickly. A move that turns out better is searched again in full.

  4. 4

    Reduce late moves

    Quiet moves far down the list are searched less deeply. If one of them surprises, it gets the full depth after all.

  5. 5

    Prune what cannot matter

    Null-move, futility and late-move pruning drop branches whose score cannot reach the window.

  6. 6

    Settle the captures, then ask the network

    At the horizon, a quiescence search plays out the captures until the position is quiet, and the neural network scores what is left.

4. Speed

Over a million nodes a second

Speed is strength at fast time controls: the AVX-512 VNNI build alone was worth +45.79 ± 14.88 Elo on the test workers (test 27). Since v6.5.1 the changes below have made the engine faster while it computes exactly what it computed before, and since 17 November the test workers build it with clang 23, where gcc 14 built it before.

5. The clock

Thinking time, spent where it pays

How much of the clock a move gets was not tuned by hand. What more thinking time is worth was measured on positions from real games, the schedule that spends the clock best was solved for, and games confirmed it.

  1. 01

    Measure what time is worth

    Positions from real test games were searched to two million nodes, with the best move noted after every iteration, and Stockfish 19 scored every move the search had considered. That shows what doubling the thinking time gains: most in moves 16 to 30, about nothing after move 50, and exactly nothing beyond ±400 centipawns.

  2. 02

    Solve for the schedule

    A dynamic program over move number and clock finds the spending that gains the most, with the lengths of 16,732 test games telling it how likely each later move is to be played. Its answer reads back as a short formula: the clock above a floor of ten increments, spread over a horizon of max(10, 42 − 1.2 × move) moves, plus nine tenths of the increment. The old formula left 4.0 of its 10 seconds unused in an average game; the schedule uses 90% of its time where the old one used 77%.

  3. 03

    Stop when the move is settled

    Within a move, the soft limit follows how long the best move has stayed the same. On 6,000 positions, another doubling gained 2.87 thousandths when the best move had just changed and 0.12 when it had held for six iterations or more, so the limit runs from 2.4 times the move's share of the clock down to half of it.

A third schedule, built from the raw measurements, put still more time early; it scored −4.74 ± 5.02 and was dropped (test 53). The method and its numbers are in pull requests #44, #45 and #47.

6. The network

An efficiently updatable neural network

A move changes only the pieces that moved, so the first layer is updated with a few vector additions instead of being recomputed. Select a layer to see what it does.

  1. Every piece on its square, seen from each side, indexed by one of 32 king buckets (HalfKAv2_hm) and by the phase of the game: three tables of 24,576, for 26 men or more, 13 to 25 and 12 or fewer. About 32 inputs are active in a position, all in its phase's table. Beside them, 55,960 relation inputs a side, of which a position lights a few dozen.

  2. The feature transformer: one int16 accumulator per side. A move adds and subtracts a few weight rows; a king move into a new bucket rebuilds that side, and a capture that takes the game into another phase rebuilds both.

  3. Side to move first, then the opponent. Each value is clamped to [0, 1] and squared, the squared clipped ReLU that gives the small layers more to work with.

  4. int8 weights. On AVX-512 VNNI hardware one instruction multiplies and accumulates 64 byte pairs. From gen768r on, this layer and the two after it exist eight times, one set for each band of four pieces on the board.

  5. int8 weights followed by another SCReLU.

  6. A score in centipawns from the side to move's point of view, which the search uses at every node it evaluates.

Six generations of one design

The accumulators have kept their shape since gen768; what changes from one generation to the next is mostly what stands behind them. Their specifications share one file in the engine repository, docs/NNUE.md, a section each. The runs up to V4 are in the development log of Luminex-NNUE, the trainer's own repository, which also covers the 512-wide networks that came before; each V5 run is in the pull request that brought its network to main (#112, #115, #123, #128, #130).

    gen768

    2 × 768 → 16 → 32 → 1

    One pair of accumulators and one small tail behind them. Its 24,576 inputs a side, its squared clipped ReLU and its output scale are still those of gen768r and gen768s. All releases from v6.1 to v6.5.1 carry a network of this shape.

  2. V2 Specified, never shipped

    gen768q

    + pawn inputs → 8 × (16 → 32 → 1)

    Written in October with v6.4 in mind: pawn-structure inputs beside the piece-square ones, passed pawns first, and eight tails chosen by the number of pieces, each addition gated by a measurement of its own. It never shipped. gen768r kept the eight tails and left the inputs as they were.

    Specification of gen768q

  3. V3 Released in v6.6.0

    gen768r

    2 × 768 → 8 × (16 → 32 → 1)

    The same inputs and accumulators, then eight tails, one for each band of four pieces on the board, each with its own int8 scales. Choosing the tail costs one popcount. Tail units that had died in earlier training were revived first. gen768r_06q, its last network, is the one inside v6.6.0, the line's only release.

    Specification of gen768r

  4. V4 Tested, not adopted

    gen768s

    … + 8 × (1,536 → 8 → 1)

    gen768r with a second, smaller head in every bucket. It predicts, in centipawns, how far the static evaluation is from what a deep search would say, and is computed only where the search's pruning margins read it. Today the search makes that guess from correction history, for a whole class of positions at once. The design allows the head 3% of the speed, and asks for an offline test and then an SPRT. The head was built and read in the pruning margins: −2.90 ± 3.59 Elo (test 146), so it stays out. gen768s_03q, main's network until 19 November, has gen768r's shape without it.

    Specification of gen768s

  5. V5 On main from 19 to 27 November

    gen768t

    + 55,960 relations → lanes 0 to 255

    gen768s's accumulators and tails, with a second table beside the piece-squares: 55,960 relation inputs a side, each a piece attacking another or a pawn attacking a pawn, added into the first 256 of the 768 lanes. Reading them costs about a fifth of the speed in games, priced at −44.36 ± 7.91 Elo at 10+0.1 (test 169); gen768t_01q still passed its SPRT against gen768s_03q at +73.37 ± 14.20 (test 174). Four more runs added +28.93 ± 9.30, +18.53 ± 7.54, +7.22 ± 4.37 and +9.86 ± 5.07 (tests 176, 195, 200 and 264); the fifth, gen768t_05q, was main's network from 23 to 27 November, and a sixth run's float network is where V6 begins. Inputs of this kind, threat inputs, other engines had first: Stockfish's Full_Threats (which credits Monty, Yukari and PlentyChess), PlentyChess, Viridithas, Stormphrax, Berserk, Caissa, Integral and Triumviratus, and Stockfish's PP_3Wide, from Pawnocchio, for the pawn pairs (#153).

    Specification of gen768t

  6. V6 Released in v6.7.0

    gen768u

    3 × 24,576 piece-squares, one table a phase

    gen768t's relation inputs, accumulators and tails, with the piece-square table held three times, once for each phase of the game, chosen by the men on the board: 26 or more, 13 to 25, 12 or fewer. Splitting the table by the count of pieces is an idea the Triumviratus engine published; Stockfish's K32H3 and nettest #531's K32Q2P4 are related. The table grows to 73,728 rows a side and costs about 2% of the speed. Its first network, gen768u_01q, passed its SPRT against gen768t_05q at +7.49 ± 4.44 (test 319), scored +6.60 ± 5.95 at 60+0.6 (test 320), and is the network inside v6.7.0.

How it is trained

Every position is scored by Leela Chess Zero's BT4 network, clipped at 1,500 centipawns. The positions come from self-play: Leela's test91 run of November 2025 to October 2026 and earlier Leela runs (test60, test77 to test80 and leela96), with one file of Stockfish's own games in the first two runs and test91's Chess960 games in V6's. The loss, the error in win probability raised to the power 3.0, counts quiet positions in full, captures at 0.3 and positions in check not at all, and a draw head learns beside it; the tails and the relation table learn four times as fast as the rest. Every network then trains 30,000 steps through the engine's own int8 rounding, with one scale for each row of the tails. Games from the end of January and positions from Stockfish's search trees are held out to judge each run.

v6.7.0's network, gen768u_01q, is the line above: 13.96 million steps from the start of V5. v6.6.0's, gen768r_06q, learned test91's own scores in 1.98 million steps. The network of v6.3 to v6.5.1 was trained by distillation: Leela positions relabeled with the score of a deep Stockfish search.

The training machine

One RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell with 96 GB of memory a run. A machine lasts ten hours, and the longer runs were fitted to end inside them. From gen768t_03 on, the trainer shuffles 2.4 billion positions on the card and keeps the rest of the data, 132 to 135 GB, in the host's memory. gen768t_01 and 02 trained at 66.6 and 66.1 steps a second; fused kernels for the relation lanes and the feature layer, and two changes shown to leave the loss as it was, took gen768t_03 and 04 to 92.8 and 95.5, and Luminex-NNUE #6, every value it computes the same, took gen768t_05 to 102.6. gen768t_06 ran at 110.56; gen768u_01, with three blocks of the piece-square table to train, at about 95 (95.30 over its last 1.25 million steps), and its 30,000 quantization-aware steps at 22.3. The code gen768t_03's and 04's runs ran is kept byte for byte in the trainer's repository (#5).
