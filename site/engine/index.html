Engine Inside

Two evaluations, one search

Luminex pairs a quantized neural network with a hand-crafted fallback, behind a search built from the techniques that define modern engines.

1. The parts

Six things it is made of

01Neural evaluation
A 768-wide HalfKAv2_hm network with 32 king buckets. Its int16 accumulators follow every move incrementally, and the int8 layers behind them run on AVX-512 VNNI or AVX2.
02Selective search
Principal variation search with aspiration windows, null-move pruning, singular extensions, ProbCut, razoring, futility and late-move pruning, and late-move reductions.
03Move ordering that learns
Killer and counter moves, continuation and capture history, static exchange evaluation, and correction history that tunes static scores from what the search finds.
04Many threads
Lazy SMP: each search thread keeps its own network accumulators and shares one transposition table with the others.
05Always a fallback
Without a network file, a hand-crafted evaluation plays instead: material, piece-square tables, mobility, passed pawns and king safety. The engine says so in an info line rather than switching quietly.
06Speaks UCI
Load it in Cute Chess, Arena, Banksia, En Croissant or any other UCI interface. MultiPV, pondering, win/draw/loss output and Polyglot opening books are built in.

2. The search

How the search spends its time

An illustration of the ideas, not a real search tree.

  1. 1

    Order the moves.

    The move stored in the hash table goes first, then winning captures, killer moves, and quiet moves ranked by their history. A good first guess makes everything after it cheaper.

  2. 2

    Search the first move fully.

    Principal variation search gives it the whole window of scores. Its result is the score every other move has to beat.

  3. 3

    Test the rest with a null window.

    A later move only has to show it is no better, and a zero-width search answers that quickly. A move that turns out better is searched again in full.

  4. 4

    Reduce late moves.

    Quiet moves far down the list are searched less deeply. If one of them surprises, it gets the full depth after all.

  5. 5

    Prune what cannot matter.

    Null-move, futility and late-move pruning drop branches whose score cannot reach the window.

  6. 6

    Settle the captures, then ask the network.

    At the horizon, a quiescence search plays out the captures until the position is quiet, and the neural network scores what is left.

3. Speed

Over a million nodes a second

Speed is strength at fast time controls: the AVX-512 VNNI build alone was worth +45.8 Elo on the test workers (test 27).

4. The clock

Thinking time, spent where it pays

How much of the clock a move gets was not tuned by hand. What more thinking time is worth was measured on positions from real games, the schedule that spends the clock best was solved for, and games confirmed it.

  1. 01

    Measure what time is worth

    Positions from real test games were searched to two million nodes, with the best move noted after every iteration, and Stockfish 19 scored every move the search had considered. That shows what doubling the thinking time gains: most in moves 16 to 30, about nothing after move 50, and exactly nothing beyond ±400 centipawns.

  2. 02

    Solve for the schedule

    A dynamic program over move number and clock finds the spending that gains the most, with the lengths of 16,732 test games telling it how likely each later move is to be played. Its answer reads back as a short formula: the clock above a floor of ten increments, spread over a horizon of max(10, 42 − 1.2 × move) moves, plus nine tenths of the increment. The old formula left 4.0 of its 10 seconds unused in an average game; the schedule uses 90% of its time where the old one used 77%.

  3. 03

    Stop when the move is settled

    Within a move, the soft limit follows how long the best move has stayed the same. On 6,000 positions, another doubling gained 2.87 thousandths when the best move had just changed and 0.12 when it had held for six iterations or more, so the limit runs from 2.4 times the move's share of the clock down to half of it.

A third schedule, built from the raw measurements, put still more time early; it lost 4.7 Elo and was dropped (test 53). The method and its numbers are in pull requests #44, #45 and #47.

5. The network

An efficiently updatable neural network

A move changes only the pieces that moved, so the first layer is updated with a few vector additions instead of being recomputed. Select a layer to see what it does.

  1. Every piece on its square, seen from each side and indexed by one of 32 king buckets (HalfKAv2_hm). About 32 of the 24,576 inputs are active in a position.

  2. The feature transformer: one int16 accumulator per side. A move adds and subtracts a few weight rows; a king move into a new bucket rebuilds that side.

  3. Side to move first, then the opponent. Each value is clamped to [0, 1] and squared, the squared clipped ReLU that gives the small layers more to work with.

  4. int8 weights. On AVX-512 VNNI hardware one instruction multiplies and accumulates 64 byte pairs. From gen768r on, this layer and the two after it exist eight times, one set for each band of four pieces on the board.

  5. int8 weights followed by another SCReLU.

  6. A score in centipawns from the side to move's point of view, which the search uses at every node it evaluates.

Four generations of one design

The accumulators have kept their shape since gen768; what changes from one generation to the next is mostly what stands behind them. Each has its specification in the repository's nnue folder, beside a development log that also covers the 512-wide networks that came before.

New in this generationSpecified, not builtCarried over

  1. V1 Released, v6.1 to v6.5.1

    gen768

    2 × 768 → 16 → 32 → 1

    One pair of accumulators and one small tail behind them. Its 24,576 inputs a side, its squared clipped ReLU and its output scale are still those of gen768r and gen768s. All releases from v6.1 to v6.5.1 carry a network of this shape.

    Specification of gen768

  2. V2 Specified, never shipped

    gen768q

    + pawn inputs → 8 × (16 → 32 → 1)

    Written in October with v6.4 in mind: pawn-structure inputs beside the piece-square ones, passed pawns first, and eight tails chosen by the number of pieces, each addition gated by a measurement of its own. It never shipped. gen768r kept the eight tails and left the inputs as they were.

    Specification of gen768q

  3. V3 On main, for 6.6

    gen768r

    2 × 768 → 8 × (16 → 32 → 1)

    The same inputs and accumulators, then eight tails, one for each band of four pieces on the board, each with its own int8 scales. Choosing the tail costs one popcount. Tail units that had died in earlier training were revived first. gen768r_04q is the network on main.

    Specification of gen768r

  4. V4 Designed, for 6.7

    gen768s

    … + 8 × (1,536 → 8 → 1)

    gen768r with a second, smaller head in every bucket. It predicts, in centipawns, how far the static evaluation is from what a deep search would say, and is computed only where the search's pruning margins read it. Today the search makes that guess from correction history, for a whole class of positions at once. The design allows the head 3% of the speed, and asks for an offline test and then an SPRT. It is a design; nothing is built yet.

    Specification of gen768s

How it is trained

By distillation: positions from Leela Chess Zero self-play, each relabeled with the score of a deep Stockfish search. The loss weights quiet positions, the ones the search actually evaluates, above captures and leaves positions in check out. The released network, luminex_sfq_i8.nnue, was trained this way.

The training machine

One RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell with 96 GB of memory. The trainer keeps 2.5 billion positions on the card and the rest in the host's memory (#61), and it runs the feature layer as dense matrix products on the card's tensor cores (#59), which took training from 21.2 to 53.7 steps a second. Run 04's 400,000 steps took about 2.2 hours. Quantization-aware training is #58, and #60 converts Leela's T91 data. The engine itself is unchanged by these.

Eight output buckets: gen768r_03

The same inputs and accumulators, with eight output buckets: a separate 16 → 32 → 1 tail for each band of four pieces on the board, at no extra cost per evaluation. Tail neurons that had died in earlier training were revived before the run.

It trained for 230,000 steps of 131,072 positions, about 30 billion samples, on 2.5 billion positions from Leela's test91 self-play, February to May 2026, scored by that run's own networks; January's games were held out to check it. Against the released network it scored +54.5 ± 14.9 Elo (test 63).

A third SPSA run of 80,000 games then retuned the search constants for it. Network and constants went into main together on 10 November: +60.08 ± 15.43 Elo over the previous main in 876 games (test 68). The retuning alone, with gen768r_03 on both sides, measured −1.81 ± 3.45 over 12,484 games (test 69): that test failed, and the constants went in with the network inside the error margin.

Trained through its own rounding: gen768r_03q

The same network trained on to 460,000 steps on the same data, then 30,000 steps of quantization-aware training, which trains through the engine's own int8 rounding. Against gen768r_03 it scored +13.39 ± 6.48 Elo over 3,296 games at 10+0.1 (test 73); at 60+0.6 the same pair stood at +9.76 ± 11.08 after 712 games when the test was stopped (test 74). With it, main stood 173 to 202 Elo above v6.5.1 in self-play at CCI's three time controls (the three matches).

More positions, more steps: gen768r_04q

It continues the same run from 460,000 to 860,000 steps, now on 3.73 billion positions from test91's February to August and October 2026 games, with a loss power of 3.0 instead of 2.6, then 30,000 quantization-aware steps. Against gen768r_03q it scored +13.31 ± 6.55 Elo over 3,394 games at 10+0.1 (test 82). It went onto main on 12 November. An SPSA run of 80,000 games then retuned 13 search margins for it (tune 83), but none moved beyond noise; the SPRT of its values was stopped (test 84) and main keeps its margins. With this network, a gauntlet against twelve rated engines put main at 3448 ± 24 on the CCRL Blitz scale (the table).

On main now: gen768r_05q

The run went on from 860,000 steps to 1,502,671, on 6.6 billion positions: test91's games of November and December 2025, 4.24 billion positions that no network of this line had trained on, beside those of February to May 2026. Then 30,000 quantization-aware steps. On January's games, which every run has left out, its loss before rounding is 4.3% below gen768r_04's. Against gen768r_04q it scored +22.02 ± 8.52 Elo over 1,912 games at 10+0.1 (test 112). Since 13 November it is the network on main (#72) and in every development build, and OpenBench's default. It has gen768r_04q's size and speed. No gauntlet has rated it yet: the 3448 ± 24 was measured with gen768r_04q.
